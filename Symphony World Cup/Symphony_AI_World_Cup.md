# Symphony AI World Cup

## What Is It

The Symphony AI World Cup is a company-wide initiative designed to drive AI adoption from the inside out. Multi-disciplinary squads tackle real Symphony business problems using AI, competing across four rounds before a Grand Final Event where a champion is crowned. The goal is not just winning — every squad that participates leaves something tangible behind: a documented business process or new solution, a working AI tool, and measured proof of value created.

---

## Objectives

- Accelerate AI adoption across the entire business
- Surface and solve real operational inefficiencies using AI, and create entirely new solutions that open up possibilities the business hasn't yet explored
- Build a company-wide library of documented business processes
- Create measurable value that compounds over time
- Develop AI capability across all disciplines, not just engineering
- Make AI enablement part of how every person at Symphony grows and is measured

---

## Season Structure

A season runs over a full year with four rounds, followed by the Grand Final Event — a standalone end-of-year celebration, not a round in itself:

- **Round 1** — approx. 10–12 weeks
- **Round 2** — approx. 10–12 weeks
- **Round 3** — approx. 10–12 weeks
- **Round 4** — approx. 10–12 weeks
- **End of Year** — Grand Final Event

Each round produces its own winner, recognised publicly and awarded a round prize. The **top three solutions from every round** advance to the Grand Final, giving up to twelve finalists in total — a wider field that rewards more squads for getting close, and brings more solutions in front of the company.

### Round Entry

Squads may enter any round and as many rounds as they choose, providing they bring a different problem to each one. Qualifying for the Grand Final does not lock a squad out of further rounds — the goal is to solve as many real problems as possible across the year.

---

## The League Table

A running season table is published and updated after every round, visible to the whole company. It tracks:

- Squads in the competition this round
- Round scores and standings
- Number of solutions advanced to the Grand Final per region
- Top-performing squads across the season

The League Table is not a competitive mechanism — there is no prize for topping it. It exists to create visibility, celebrate participation, and keep the energy alive between rounds. Region managers are encouraged to reference it in their internal communications.

## Squad Composition

Every squad must include at least one person from each of the following disciplines:

- **Design** — ensures the solution is usable and will actually be adopted
- **Product** — ensures the solution solves the right problem and impact is measurable
- **Engineering or Data** — ensures the solution is technically feasible and robust
- **Business SME** — the domain expert who knows the as-is process, validates the problem, and signs off on results

**Minimum 4 people. Maximum 5.**

### The SME Rule

The SME is not a passive advisor — they are a core squad member. They bring the domain knowledge the other disciplines cannot replicate, validate that results are genuine, and become the natural champion for scaling the solution within the business after the competition. Where a problem comes from the Pitch Board, the person who submitted that problem becomes the natural SME for whichever squad picks it.

For process improvement squads, the SME's primary role is documenting the as-is state and validating that the measured results are real. For net new idea squads, the SME acts as the business authority — the person who can credibly argue that the opportunity is genuine, that the solution addresses it, and that the results demonstrate real value.

### If a Discipline Is Missing

Region managers facilitate a volunteer matching pool before each round. Individuals from Design, Product, and Engineering who want to participate but don't have a squad can register as available to be matched. If a squad genuinely cannot find a person from one discipline after exhausting the pool, they may substitute that one discipline with AI — but must explicitly demonstrate during their pitch how AI covered that role. A squad may never substitute more than one discipline.

### AI Is Mandatory

Every squad must use AI as a core part of their solution, not just mention it. How AI was used is part of the judging criteria. Squads are also encouraged to use AI in their pitch preparation — to stress-test their business case, simulate Scout questions, and sharpen their narrative.

---

## The Squad Coach

Every squad is assigned a Squad Coach — a senior Symphony employee who is not a player on the squad but acts as a mentor and performance supporter throughout the round. The Squad Coach is separate from the region manager, whose role is organisational. The Coach is about squad performance.

### What a Squad Coach Does

- Meets with the squad at the start of the round to help scope the problem and sharpen the brief
- Is available as a sounding board during the build phase
- Runs **The Friendly** — a pre-match pressure test before Match Day where the Coach challenges the squad's numbers, questions their assumptions, and prepares them for tough Scout questions
- Advocates for the squad's solution internally if it has potential beyond the competition

### Who Can Be a Squad Coach

Any senior employee who is not competing in that round. Region managers may volunteer as coaches for squads outside their region. Previous round winners are encouraged to coach in subsequent rounds.

---

## Central Ownership

The AI World Cup is owned centrally by the Community of Practice lead, who is accountable for the overall initiative across all seasons. The central owner is responsible for:

- Maintaining and publishing the Pitch Board before each season
- Running the Track B approval panel for open pitch submissions
- Setting and communicating the judging criteria and pitch framework each round
- Organising Match Days and the Grand Final Event
- Publishing results, scores, and Match Reports after each round
- Overseeing the fast-track implementation process for winning solutions
- Evolving the rules and format between seasons based on what is learned

Region managers operate within the framework the central owner sets. They do not make structural decisions — those sit centrally.

---

## Season Kickoff

Each season follows a defined launch sequence before Round 1 begins:

1. **Six weeks before Round 1** — the central owner announces the new season company-wide. The Pitch Board opens for department submissions. Region managers are briefed on their responsibilities and participation targets.
2. **Four weeks before Round 1** — the Pitch Board closes for submissions. Leadership validates and scopes entries. The final Pitch Board is published.
3. **Two weeks before Round 1** — squad registration opens. The matching pool opens for individuals without a squad. Track B problem submissions accepted.
4. **One week before Round 1** — Track B submissions reviewed and responded to within 48 hours. Squad rosters finalised and submitted to region managers.
5. **Round 1 begins.**

## The Regional Model

Region managers own the initiative in their territory. They are organisers and champions, not competitors. There is no regional leaderboard or regional scoring — all squads compete in one global pool regardless of where they are based.

### What Region Managers Do

- Drive squad recruitment and ensure every squad in their region is properly formed before the round begins
- Facilitate the matching pool to connect squads with missing disciplines
- Coordinate Squad Coach assignments for their region
- Maintain momentum throughout each round — internal Slack updates, shoutouts, League Table references, keeping the energy up
- Show up publicly and visibly on Match Day to champion their squads

### The Recognition

If a squad from a region wins, that is the region manager's moment too. They championed those people, drove their involvement, and their region produced the champion. That is recognised publicly.

## Problem Sourcing — The Pitch Board

Squads choose their problem through one of two tracks:

### Track A — The Pitch Board

A curated backlog of real Symphony business problems and How Might We (HMW) statements, built from the bottom up. Before each season, every department is asked to contribute problems or HMW statements from their area — the people closest to the work are best placed to identify where AI can make a difference. Leadership validates and scopes submissions to ensure they are achievable within a single round. Each problem on the Pitch Board comes with a named SME attached — typically the person from that department who submitted it. Squads pick a problem and that SME joins their squad.

### Track B — Open Pitch

Squads identify their own problem. Before the round begins they submit a one-paragraph problem statement for approval. A small panel reviews and approves or redirects within 48 hours. Squads then find their own SME from within that part of the business.

Both tracks compete on equal terms. Judging criteria do not favour either.

## The Round

Each round runs for approximately 10 to 12 weeks. Squads work part-time alongside their day jobs, with AI doing the heavy execution work. Each round is structured in four phases:

| Phase | Timing | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Weeks 1–2 | Squads confirmed, problem locked. Process improvement squads begin as-is documentation. Net new squads define the opportunity and establish how value will be measured. |
| Build | Weeks 3–6 | Solution designed and built with AI. First test run completed on real data or workflows. Mid-point check-in with region manager — brief Slack update or 10-minute call. |
| Results | Weeks 7–9 | Solution running. Real results being gathered. Pitch preparation begins. |
| Match Prep | Weeks 10–12 | The Friendly with Squad Coach. Final refinements. Match Day. |

### Match Day Format

Each squad receives:
- **10 minutes** to present
- **5 minutes** Q&A from the Scouts

---

## What Squads Must Deliver

Three elements are mandatory for all squads. A fourth element applies depending on the type of solution. Missing any mandatory element disqualifies the submission.

### 1. The Working Solution *(all squads)*
Built, deployed, and running. Not a prototype. Not a mockup. Something that actually worked during the round on real data or real workflows.

### 2. Measured Results *(all squads)*
Evidence of real value created, drawn from actual usage during the round. Results must be quantified — time saved, errors reduced, cost removed, new capability demonstrated. The SME validates the numbers. Projected or modelled results are not acceptable.

### 3. The New State *(all squads)*
A clear picture of how things now work with the AI solution in place — whether that is an improved process or a new capability that didn't exist before. Clear enough that someone outside the squad could understand and operate it.

### 4a. As-Is Process Documentation *(process improvement squads only)*
How did this process work before? Who did it, how long did it take, where were the pain points, what did it cost? This is the baseline that makes the measured results credible. The SME leads this. Without it, the delta cannot be verified.

### 4b. Opportunity Definition *(net new idea squads only)*
What gap, pain point, or opportunity did the squad identify? Why does it matter to the business? What was the evidence that this was worth solving? This replaces the as-is documentation for squads building something that did not previously exist.

## The Judging Panel — The Scouts

### The Scouts

A panel of four judges, each bringing a distinct lens:

- **The Sporting Director** — senior Symphony leadership. Focused on business impact. Do the numbers stack up? Is this a real problem worth solving?
- **The Technical Director** — engineering or data leadership. Can this actually work? Is it robust? Does it scale?
- **The Commercial Director** — consulting or commercial leadership. Could this be packaged for clients? Would the market pay for it?
- **The Guest Scout** — rotates each round. Could be an external guest, a client, an industry figure, or the previous round's winning squad captain.

For the Grand Final, the panel becomes more senior and includes at least one external Scout.

### How Judging Works

Each Scout scores independently. Scores are only revealed after every squad has pitched — no earlier scores anchor later judgements. After all pitches, the Scouts deliberate briefly and each names their top pick publicly with their reasoning. The top three solutions from the round are confirmed and the round winner is announced.

### Round Scoring Criteria

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Measurable business impact | 40% |
| Clarity and credibility of the problem definition and baseline | 20% |
| AI innovation and creativity | 25% |
| Presentation clarity | 15% |

*The second criterion applies to both solution types: for process improvement squads it assesses the quality of the as-is documentation; for net new squads it assesses how well the opportunity was defined and evidenced.*

### Grand Final Scoring Criteria

The Grand Final Event judges the finalist solutions against each other. Since solutions may address completely different problems, the Scout criteria shift to assess the quality, significance, and scalability of each solution rather than comparing raw numbers directly. Symphony staff also cast a live vote as part of the final decision.

| Criteria | Notes |
|---|---|
| Credibility and scale of measured results | Scouts |
| Business significance of the problem solved | Scouts |
| Scalability across the wider business | Scouts |
| AI innovation and creativity | Scouts |
| Symphony staff vote | Live vote during the event — every employee participates |

The exact weighting of Scout scores versus staff vote is set by the central owner before the Grand Final and published in advance.

## Helping Squads Do Their Best

### Pitch Framework
Every squad receives a pitch framework at the start of each round that sets out what the Scouts expect to see. The framework is a guide, not a rigid template — squads shape their own narrative within it. How it is applied depends on the nature of the problem:

**For process improvement problems** — squads document the as-is state, show what they built, present the measured delta, and explain how it scales.

**For net new ideas** — squads define the opportunity or gap they identified, demonstrate that their solution works and creates real value, and show evidence of that value through usage, feedback, or early results.

In both cases, squads are responsible for building their own story. The framework tells them what questions the Scouts will be asking — it does not write the answers for them.

### Published Question Bank
A set of the kinds of questions Scouts are likely to ask is shared upfront. Squads who have never pitched to a senior panel should not be disadvantaged because they did not know what to expect. Example questions:
- How did you establish the as-is baseline?
- How long did the solution actually run and on what data?
- What happens if this breaks?
- Who owns this after today?
- What would it cost to scale across the business?

### The Friendly
Before Match Day, every squad runs The Friendly — a pre-match pressure test with their Squad Coach. The Coach challenges the numbers, questions assumptions, and asks the uncomfortable questions that Scouts will ask in the room. Squads should not hear those questions for the first time on Match Day.

### AI Pitch Preparation
Squads are explicitly encouraged to use AI to prepare their pitch. Simulate Scout questions. Pressure-test the business case. Refine the narrative. The best squads use AI throughout — not just in the solution, but in how they communicate it.

### Match Report
Every squad, not just winners, receives a written Match Report from the Scouts within 48 hours of Match Day. What worked, what did not, what would have changed the score. Squads that do not advance use this to come back stronger the following round.

## The Grand Final Event

The Grand Final is the centrepiece of the season — a company-wide event held at the end of the year where the finalists (top three from each of the four rounds) compete for the championship. It is not a round. No new solutions are built. Squads present the solution they qualified with, updated with any additional results gathered since.

### Format
- Held in person where possible, with a company-wide livestream for those not attending
- Pitch format set by the central owner based on finalist count
- Pitch order determined by random draw on the day
- Full Scout panel including at least one external guest — a client, investor, or industry figure
- All finalists present before any deliberation begins
- **Symphony staff vote** opens during the event — every employee can vote for the solution they think should win
- Scouts deliberate live, each naming their pick and reasoning publicly
- The combined Scout score and staff vote determine the champion
- Winner announced at the event

### Audience
The Grand Final is a company-wide moment. All Symphony employees are encouraged to attend or watch — and to vote. Leadership attendance is expected. The event should feel like a genuine celebration of what the business has built across the year.

---

## The Prize

### Round Prize
Each round has its own winner, recognised and rewarded immediately. The round prize is meaningful but smaller — a personal prize for each squad member and a public company-wide announcement. Winning a round is a real achievement, not just a stepping stone.

### Grand Final Prize
The Grand Final is where the season's biggest prize lives. Three layers:

**The Trophy** — a physical, permanent trophy that lives with the winning squad's region. Bragging rights that last beyond the season.

**The Personal Prize** — something significant for each member of the Grand Final winning squad. A trip, a learning budget, or tech gear — something worth competing hard for.

**The Real Prize** — the winning solution gets resourced and actually implemented across the business. Within two weeks of the Grand Final, the central owner initiates a fast-track implementation process. The winning squad's SME leads adoption within their part of the business, supported by Symphony leadership. Progress is reported back to the company.

## AI and Performance Management

Participation in the AI World Cup is one expression of a broader commitment: AI enablement is becoming part of how every person at Symphony is expected to grow and is measured. A four-level AI capability framework applies across Product, Engineering, Data, and other disciplines:

- **Level 1** — AI aware: understands what AI can and cannot do
- **Level 2** — AI assisted: uses AI tools regularly in day-to-day work
- **Level 3** — AI enabled: builds solutions and workflows using AI
- **Level 4** — AI led: shapes how AI is adopted and applied across teams and clients

The AI World Cup is the company's practical proving ground for Levels 3 and 4. Competing, building, and shipping a real AI solution is one of the clearest demonstrations that an employee has moved beyond awareness and into genuine capability.

---

## Time Commitment

Squads are working part-time alongside full-time responsibilities. The expected commitment is approximately 2 to 3 hours per person per week during a round. This is made possible by AI doing the heavy execution work — the squad's job is to direct it, not manually build everything from scratch.

Leadership must visibly protect this time. Participation in the AI World Cup is legitimate work, not an after-hours personal project. Region managers communicate this clearly in their regions at the start of each season.

---

## The Compounding Effect

Every season of the AI World Cup leaves Symphony with more than a champion. It leaves a growing library of documented business processes, a set of working AI solutions with proven results, a cohort of employees who have shipped something real with AI, and a measurable picture of where AI is creating value across the business — which becomes a powerful story to tell internally and externally over time.
