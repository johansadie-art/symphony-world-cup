# Symphony AI World Cup

## What Is It

The Symphony AI World Cup is a company-wide initiative designed to drive AI adoption from the inside out. Multi-disciplinary teams tackle real Symphony business problems using AI, competing across four quarterly rounds before a Grand Final Event where a champion is crowned. The goal is not just winning — every team that participates leaves something tangible behind: a documented business process or new solution, a working AI tool, and measured proof of value created.

---

## Objectives

- Accelerate AI adoption across the entire business
- Surface and solve real operational inefficiencies using AI, and create entirely new solutions that open up possibilities the business hasn't yet explored
- Build a company-wide library of documented business processes
- Create measurable value that compounds over time
- Develop AI capability across all disciplines, not just engineering

---

## Season Structure

A season runs over a full year with four quarterly rounds, followed by the Grand Final — a standalone end-of-year event, not a round in itself:

- **Q1** — Round 1
- **Q2** — Round 2
- **Q3** — Round 3
- **Q4** — Round 4
- **End of Year** — Grand Final Event

Each round produces its own winner, recognised publicly and awarded a round prize. The **top three solutions from every round** advance to the Grand Final, giving twelve finalists in total — a wider field that rewards more teams for getting close, and brings more solutions in front of the company.

### Round Entry

Teams may enter any round and as many rounds as they choose, providing they bring a different problem to each one. Qualifying for the Grand Final does **not** lock a team out of further rounds — the goal is to solve as many real problems as possible across the year. A team that qualifies with one solution and then ranks in the top three again with a different problem brings both solutions to the Grand Final.

## Teams

### Composition

Every team must include at least one person from each of the following disciplines:

- **Design** — ensures the solution is usable and will actually be adopted
- **Product** — ensures the solution solves the right problem and impact is measurable
- **Engineering or Data** — ensures the solution is technically feasible and robust
- **Business SME** — the domain expert who knows the as-is process, validates the problem, and signs off on results

**Minimum 4 people. Maximum 5.**

### The SME Rule

The SME is not a passive advisor — they are a core team member. They bring the domain knowledge the other disciplines cannot replicate, validate that results are genuine, and become the natural champion for scaling the solution within the business after the competition. Where a problem comes from the Problem Bank, the person who submitted that problem becomes the natural SME for whichever team picks it.

For process improvement teams, the SME's primary role is documenting the as-is state and validating that the measured results are real. For net new idea teams, the SME acts as the business authority — the person who can credibly argue that the opportunity is genuine, that the solution addresses it, and that the results demonstrate real value.

### If a Discipline Is Missing

Region managers facilitate a volunteer matching pool before each round. Individuals from Design, Product, and Engineering who want to participate but don't have a team can register as available to be matched. If a team genuinely cannot find a person from one discipline after exhausting the pool, they may substitute that one discipline with AI — but must explicitly demonstrate during their pitch how AI covered that role. A team may never substitute more than one discipline.

### AI Is Mandatory

Every team must use AI as a core part of their solution, not just mention it. How AI was used is part of the judging criteria. Teams are also encouraged to use AI in their pitch preparation — to stress-test their business case, simulate judge questions, and sharpen their narrative.

---

## Central Ownership

The AI World Cup is owned centrally by the Community of Practice lead, who is accountable for the overall initiative across all seasons. The central owner is responsible for:

- Maintaining and publishing the Problem Bank before each season
- Running the Track B approval panel for open pitch submissions
- Setting and communicating the judging criteria and pitch framework each round
- Organising Demo Days and the Grand Final Event
- Publishing results, scores, and Dragon feedback after each round
- Overseeing the fast-track implementation process for winning solutions
- Evolving the rules and format between seasons based on what is learned

Region managers operate within the framework the central owner sets. They do not make structural decisions — those sit centrally.

---

## Season Kickoff

Each season follows a defined launch sequence before Round 1 begins:

1. **Six weeks before Round 1** — the central owner announces the new season company-wide. The Problem Bank opens for department submissions. Region managers are briefed on their responsibilities and participation targets.
2. **Four weeks before Round 1** — the Problem Bank closes for submissions. Leadership validates and scopes entries. The final Problem Bank is published.
3. **Two weeks before Round 1** — team registration opens. The matching pool opens for individuals without a team. Track B problem submissions accepted.
4. **One week before Round 1** — Track B submissions reviewed and responded to within 48 hours. Team rosters finalised and submitted to region managers.
5. **Round 1 begins.**

## The Regional Model

Region managers own the initiative in their territory. They are organisers and champions, not competitors. There is no regional leaderboard or regional scoring — all teams compete in one global pool regardless of where they are based.

### What Region Managers Do

- Drive team recruitment and ensure every team in their region is properly formed before the round begins
- Facilitate the matching pool to connect teams with missing disciplines
- Maintain momentum throughout each round — internal Slack updates, shoutouts, keeping the energy up
- Run a dry-run pitch session with each of their teams before demo day
- Show up publicly and visibly on demo day to champion their teams

### The Recognition

If a team from a region wins, that is the region manager's moment too. They championed those people, drove their involvement, and their region produced the champion. That is recognised publicly.

## Problem Sourcing

Teams choose their problem through one of two tracks:

### Track A — The Problem Bank

A curated backlog of real Symphony business problems and How Might We (HMW) statements, built from the bottom up. Before each season, every department is asked to contribute problems or HMW statements from their area — the people closest to the work are best placed to identify where AI can make a difference. Leadership validates and scopes submissions to ensure they are achievable within a single round. Each problem in the bank comes with a named SME attached — typically the person from that department who submitted it. Teams pick a problem and that SME joins their team.

### Track B — Open Pitch

Teams identify their own problem. Before the round begins they submit a one-paragraph problem statement for approval. A small panel reviews and approves or redirects within 48 hours. Teams then find their own SME from within that part of the business.

Both tracks compete on equal terms. Judging criteria do not favour either.

## The Quarterly Round

Each round runs across a full quarter — approximately 10 to 12 weeks. Teams work part-time alongside their day jobs, with AI doing the heavy execution work. The quarter is structured in four phases:

| Phase | Timing | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Weeks 1–2 | Teams confirmed, problem locked. Process improvement teams begin as-is documentation. Net new teams define the opportunity and establish how value will be measured. |
| Build | Weeks 3–6 | Solution designed and built with AI. First test run completed on real data or workflows. Mid-point check-in with region manager — brief Slack update or 10-minute call. |
| Results | Weeks 7–9 | Solution running. Real results being gathered. Pitch preparation begins. |
| Demo | Weeks 10–12 | Region manager dry-run pitch. Final refinements. Demo Day. |

### Demo Day Format

Each team receives:
- **10 minutes** to present
- **5 minutes** Q&A from the Dragons

## What Teams Must Deliver

Three elements are mandatory for all teams. A fourth element applies depending on the type of solution. Missing any mandatory element disqualifies the submission.

### 1. The Working Solution *(all teams)*
Built, deployed, and running. Not a prototype. Not a mockup. Something that actually worked during the round on real data or real workflows.

### 2. Measured Results *(all teams)*
Evidence of real value created, drawn from actual usage during the round. Results must be quantified — time saved, errors reduced, cost removed, new capability demonstrated. The SME validates the numbers. Projected or modelled results are not acceptable.

### 3. The New State *(all teams)*
A clear picture of how things now work with the AI solution in place — whether that is an improved process or a new capability that didn't exist before. Clear enough that someone outside the team could understand and operate it.

### 4a. As-Is Process Documentation *(process improvement teams only)*
How did this process work before? Who did it, how long did it take, where were the pain points, what did it cost? This is the baseline that makes the measured results credible. The SME leads this. Without it, the delta cannot be verified.

### 4b. Opportunity Definition *(net new idea teams only)*
What gap, pain point, or opportunity did the team identify? Why does it matter to the business? What was the evidence that this was worth solving? This replaces the as-is documentation for teams building something that did not previously exist.

## The Judging Panel — Dragons' Den

### The Dragons

A panel of four judges, each bringing a distinct lens:

- **The Business Dragon** — senior Symphony leadership. Focused on business impact. Do the numbers stack up? Is this a real problem worth solving?
- **The Technical Dragon** — engineering or data leadership. Can this actually work? Is it robust? Does it scale?
- **The Client Dragon** — consulting or commercial leadership. Could this be packaged for clients? Would the market pay for it?
- **The Wildcard Dragon** — rotates each round. Could be an external guest, a client, an industry figure, or the previous round's winning team captain.

For the Grand Final, the panel becomes more senior and includes at least one external Dragon.

### How Judging Works

Each Dragon scores independently. Scores are only revealed after every team has pitched — no earlier scores anchor later judgements. After all pitches, the Dragons deliberate briefly and each names their top pick publicly with their reasoning. The winner emerges from that conversation.

### Round Scoring Criteria

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Measurable business impact | 40% |
| Clarity and credibility of the problem definition and baseline | 20% |
| AI innovation and creativity | 25% |
| Presentation clarity | 15% |

*The second criterion applies to both solution types: for process improvement teams it assesses the quality of the as-is documentation; for net new teams it assesses how well the opportunity was defined and evidenced.*

### Grand Final Scoring Criteria

The Grand Final Event judges the twelve finalist solutions against each other. Since solutions may address completely different problems, the Dragon criteria shift to assess the quality, significance, and scalability of each solution rather than comparing raw numbers directly. Symphony staff also vote as part of the final decision.

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Credibility and scale of measured results (Dragons) | Published before the event |
| Business significance of the problem solved (Dragons) | Published before the event |
| Scalability across the wider business (Dragons) | Published before the event |
| AI innovation and creativity (Dragons) | Published before the event |
| Symphony staff vote | Included in the final decision |

## Helping Teams Do Their Best

### Pitch Framework
Every team receives a pitch framework at the start of each round that sets out what the Dragons expect to see. The framework is a guide, not a rigid template — teams shape their own narrative within it. How it is applied depends on the nature of the problem:

**For process improvement problems** — where an existing workflow is being enhanced, teams are expected to document the as-is state, show what they built, present the measured delta, and explain how it scales.

**For net new ideas** — where nothing exists today to compare against, teams define the opportunity or gap they identified, demonstrate that their solution works and creates real value, and show evidence of that value through usage, feedback, or early results. There is no as-is to document, but the case for why this matters must be made clearly.

In both cases, teams are responsible for building their own story. The framework tells them what questions the Dragons will be asking — it does not write the answers for them.

### Published Question Bank
A set of the kinds of questions Dragons are likely to ask is shared upfront. Teams who have never pitched to a senior panel should not be disadvantaged because they did not know what to expect. Example questions:
- How did you establish the as-is baseline?
- How long did the solution actually run and on what data?
- What happens if this breaks?
- Who owns this after today?
- What would it cost to scale across the business?

### Region Manager Dry Run
Before demo day, every team pitches once to their region manager. The region manager's job is to play Devil's Dragon — challenge the numbers, poke at feasibility, ask uncomfortable questions. Teams should not hear those questions for the first time in the room.

### AI Pitch Preparation
Teams are explicitly encouraged to use AI to prepare their pitch. Simulate judge questions. Pressure-test the business case. Refine the narrative. The best teams use AI throughout — not just in the solution, but in how they communicate it.

### Post-Round Feedback
Every team, not just winners, receives written feedback from the Dragons within 48 hours of demo day. What worked, what did not, what would have changed the score. Teams that do not advance use this to come back stronger the following round.

## The Grand Final Event

The Grand Final is the centrepiece of the season — a company-wide event held at the end of Q4 where the **twelve finalists** (top three from each of the four rounds) compete for the championship. It is not a round. No new solutions are built. Teams present the solution they qualified with, optionally updated with any additional results gathered since.

### Format
- Held in person where possible, with a company-wide livestream for those not attending
- Pitch format is set by the central owner based on finalist count (for example, a shorter format when all twelve finalists pitch)
- Pitch order determined by random draw on the day
- Full Dragon panel including at least one external guest — a client, investor, or industry figure
- All twelve teams present before any deliberation begins
- **Symphony staff vote** opens during the event — every employee can vote for the solution they think should win
- Dragons deliberate live, each naming their pick and reasoning publicly
- The combined Dragon score and staff vote determine the champion (see Grand Final Scoring Criteria)
- Winner announced at the event

### Audience
The Grand Final is a company-wide moment. All Symphony employees are encouraged to attend or watch — and to vote. Leadership attendance is expected. The event should feel like a genuine celebration of what the business has built across the year — not just a competition finale.

---

## The Prize

### Round Prize
Each round has its own winner, recognised and rewarded immediately. The round prize is meaningful but smaller — a personal prize for each team member and a public company-wide announcement. Winning a round is a real achievement, not just a stepping stone.

### Grand Final Prize
The Grand Final is where the season's biggest prize lives. Three layers:

**The Trophy** — a physical, permanent trophy that lives with the winning team's region. Bragging rights that last beyond the season.

**The Personal Prize** — something significant for each member of the Grand Final winning team. A trip, a learning budget, or tech gear — something worth competing hard for.

**The Real Prize** — the winning solution gets resourced and actually implemented across the business. Within two weeks of the Grand Final, the central owner initiates a fast-track implementation process. The winning team's SME leads adoption within their part of the business, supported by Symphony leadership. Progress is reported back to the company. This is the most powerful motivator — people work hardest when they know their solution has a genuine shot at becoming the new way Symphony operates.

---

## Time Commitment

Teams are working part-time alongside full-time responsibilities. The expected commitment is approximately 2 to 3 hours per person per week during a round. This is made possible by AI doing the heavy execution work — the team's job is to direct it, not manually build everything from scratch.

Leadership must visibly protect this time. Participation in the AI World Cup is legitimate work, not an after-hours personal project. Region managers communicate this clearly in their regions at the start of each season.

---

## The Compounding Effect

Every season of the AI World Cup leaves Symphony with more than a champion. It leaves a growing library of documented business processes, a set of working AI solutions with proven results, a cohort of employees who have shipped something real with AI, and a measurable picture of where AI is creating incremental gains across the business — which becomes a powerful story to tell internally and externally over time.
